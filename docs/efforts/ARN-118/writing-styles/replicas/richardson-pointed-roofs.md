The jars stood in a row along the scrubbed table, still warm from the oven. Nora counted them over. Nine, and the tenth not filled. The kitchen smelt of scorched sugar and of the wet flags by the door, and above her the house was quite silent; Aunt Clarke had gone up to lie down.

She lifted the preserving-pan with both hands. It was heavier than it looked, and the skin on the back of her wrist drew tight where the steam had caught it in the morning. Out in the yard someone was dragging a zinc bath over the cobbles. The plums had gone a dark clouded red, not the red they had been in the basket. Sunday afternoons at home the copper was always going and there was that smell of hot linen on the stairs and nobody speaking until four o'clock.... She poured, and the last of the fruit came out in a lump and sat in the jar refusing to settle.

Wax. There was no wax. She had seen the packet on the dresser yesterday, or on the window-sill, or perhaps that had been the string.

The clock in the passage struck, and a sound went on behind the striking, thin, running down. Three. It was much later than three. Someone was talking in the yard now, a woman's voice going up at the ends of things, and then a laugh, and then the gate.

Nora stood with the spoon in her hand looking at the window. The panes were streaming and the garden beyond them was all green movement without edges. If she rubbed it clear she would see the beans and the shed and the line of grey palings, and it was better not.... She rubbed a little of it with her cuff and looked. There was nothing out there but the beans, dripping.

The tenth jar could stand empty. Nobody counted them but herself.
