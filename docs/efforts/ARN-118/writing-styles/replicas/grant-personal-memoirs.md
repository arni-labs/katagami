On the 14th of December, 1866, I was ordered to march from Fort Randall with four companies of infantry and one of cavalry, and to open communication with the post at Cedar Bluff, which had sent no dispatch since the 2d. My command numbered three hundred and forty men present for duty. We had six wagons, forage for twelve days, and two mountain howitzers which I took against the advice of my quartermaster.

There were two routes. The river road followed the west bank for ninety-four miles and was level, but it crossed four ravines which were known to drift full after a wind. The upland trail was a hundred and eleven miles, and dry, but it carried no wood and no water between the Little Cheyenne and the Bluff, a distance of forty-one miles. I took the river road. I judged that the drifts could be dug out with the men I had, and that the loss of a day in digging was less than the certainty of a night on the open prairie without fuel.

The first eleven miles were made on the 15th in four hours. On the 16th we made nineteen miles. On the 17th the wind came from the north-west and we made four miles and camped in timber. The howitzers were of no service at any time on the march and cost me the labor of eight men and four mules for eight days. I should have left them at Randall.

We came up to Cedar Bluff on the morning of the 22d. The post had lost no men. Lieutenant Harmon, who commanded it in the absence of Captain Ives, had put his whole garrison of sixty-two into the two stone buildings on the 3d, had killed and salted the beef herd rather than try to hold it, and had sent out no couriers after the first two failed to return. His dispatches were therefore stopped by his own decision and not by the enemy. I approved what he had done. A courier lost is a man lost and tells the enemy where the post is weak, and Harmon had judged that correctly at nineteen years of age.

We remained at the Bluff nine days, repaired the stockade on the north face, and returned to Randall by the same road, arriving on the 4th of January with two men frozen in the feet and no other casualty.
