It was the last day of October, and I had been three weeks at the head of Coquetdale with my cousin Tam, doing nothing but walk the hills and eat a great deal too much. About four in the afternoon the shepherd's boy came down to the door with word that his mother had been taken badly in the night, and that the nearest doctor was at Rothbury.

I got out the map and worked the thing out. The road runs down the water by Barrowburn and Alwinton to Harbottle, and from Harbottle to Rothbury is another eight miles, which made sixteen in all, and good road for a car. But there was no car nearer than Alwinton, and the burn had been in spate since the Tuesday.

I knew that the Coquet was the trouble. Two of the fords would be running four feet, and the footbridge below Barrowburn had gone in the summer floods and had never been mended. That left the track over the law, which was three miles longer, but kept the high ground the whole way, and I could not see that a man had any choice in the matter.

I told Tam what I meant to do, and started at half-past four with a stick and no coat, for it is easier to be wet than to be hampered. The first two miles up the shoulder were the worst of it. There was a wind off the Cheviots with sleet in it, and the peat hags were full, and twice I went in to the thigh.

By six I was on the top and could see the lights of Alwinton away to the south-east, maybe four miles off. I came down by a sheep-drain into the road and did the rest of it at a jog-trot. I have never been much of a runner, but a man will go a long way when there is a thing to be done and nobody else to do it.

The doctor was out at Holystone and did not get back till nine. That was the one thing I had not reckoned on, and it cost us the best part of two hours; and by the time we had the trap on the road the sleet had turned to snow, and I was beginning to think about the ford at Linshiels.
