Now tells the tale of Ormar the son of Hallstein, who dwelt north in Raumsdale, that he had two sons by his wife Ingibiorg, and the elder was called Kolbein, and the younger Ari, and both were great of growth and hardy of heart, howsoever men deemed the younger the better man at weapons.

There was a man hight Gunnlaug, a rich bonder, and he held the grazing above the river, and thereof arose the quarrel; for on a summer night his herdsmen drove Ormar's sheep down into the marsh, and there the more part of them were drowned, and Ari counted them in the morning and told his father thereof.

Then spake Ormar, "Sheep are but sheep, and the marsh is old; yet if a man take from me twice, he shall know well that I sleep not; wherefore ride ye to Gunnlaug and bid him pay, and be soft of speech until he answers, and after his answer be what ye will."

So the brethren rode over the heath, and came to the garth at evening, and Gunnlaug stood in his doorway with a cup in his hand, and would not hear them out, but said--

"Thy father's sheep went whither thy father's wits went; get thee home, lad."

Ari said naught. Kolbein said that the word should be told at home even as it was spoken, and thereat they turned their horses and rode back, and it was told even so.

Now the winter wore away, and men said the matter was dead. But in the spring Gunnlaug fared to the Thing with eleven men, and the sons of Ormar lay in the birch wood above the ford, and when the company was come into the water, Ari shot the foremost horse, and thereafter they set on with sword and axe, and the fight was short. Gunnlaug was slain there, and four men with him, and one of Ormar's house was hurt in the thigh, and no more. Then they rode home and told their father, and he asked how many stood yet on the other side, and Kolbein said seven.

"That is over many," said Ormar, "for seven men remember well."
