The road out of Socorro climbs for nine miles before it gives you anything, and then it gives you the whole of the valley at once. I had been told of this by a man on the train, and had not believed him, easterners being commonly lied to for sport in that country; but he had understated it. Below and behind lay the river, brown and unhurried between its cottonwoods, and westward the plain ran out flat and pale to a line of blue mountains that did not appear to be mountains at all, but a low bank of weather. There was no fence in any direction. A hawk hung above the road and did not trouble to move as we came under it.

At the water hole where the freighters stop, a man was shoeing a mule. He had the near hind foot in his lap and his hat on the ground beside him, and he worked without haste, laying the nails in a row upon his knee and driving them one at a time. Two other mules stood loose and unhobbled and watched him. When he had clinched the last nail he set the foot down, straightened himself, and looked at us for the first time.

"Yu' come up from the river?" he said.

I said that we had.

"Then yu' passed my wagon."

The driver of our stage remarked that we had passed a wagon with a broken tongue, some four miles back, and a man asleep beneath it.

"That'd be Cleve," said the mule-shoer. He picked up his hat and knocked the dust out of it against his leg. "He's been asleep since Toosday. It's a gift."

I asked him, being a stranger and free to ask foolish things, whether he did not mean to go back and lend a hand.

He considered this for as long as it takes to fill a pipe. "Well," he said, "I told him about that tongue at Lemitar."

Nobody laughed, and I understood that nothing had been said which was meant to be funny. The driver went off to water his team. The mule-shoer put on his hat, took up the next foot, and the little ringing of his hammer began again behind us as we pulled out upon the flat.
