There is no surer index of a man's character than the things he laughs at. I have repeated this to myself for years, and repeated it to others with the confidence of a person who has never once looked into the grounds of his own opinion; and it is only lately, in turning over some old acquaintance in my memory, that I have begun to doubt it. There was a bookseller in Holborn whom I knew for eleven years, a hard man in a bargain and a harder one in an argument, who laughed at nothing in the world but the misfortunes of the great; and yet I never saw him refuse credit to a poor scholar, nor heard him ask for it back. His laughter and his conduct were two different men living in the same coat. On the other hand, I have known a mild, obliging, universally esteemed gentleman who was never heard to laugh at anything crueller than a pun, and who ruined his brother's family with the same smooth countenance that he wore at breakfast. What are we to make of the test, then? It holds, I think, only in company. A man in a room with six others laughs to be seen laughing; he takes the tone of the table as a fiddler takes his pitch from the man next him, and what you learn from him is not his nature but his neighbourhood. It is when he is alone that his laughter tells. I have watched a stranger reading by himself in the corner of a coffee-house, and seen him smile at a page that nobody had recommended to him, and known more of him in that half-minute than his friends could have told me in a twelvemonth. 'The rest is silence,' and the silence is the whole of the matter. So I would put the claim as I now find it, having lost the half of it on the way: a man's laughter betrays him only when there is nobody by to hear it; and the moralists who go about listening in company for the key to a character are gathering echoes, and setting them down as voices.
