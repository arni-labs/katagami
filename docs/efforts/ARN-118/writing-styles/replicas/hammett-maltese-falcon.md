Rain had been falling on Dearborn Street since eleven o'clock. A dark green sedan stood at the curb below the Marchand Building with its lights off and its motor running. The man behind the wheel wore a grey cap and a tan raincoat buttoned to the chin. He looked at his wrist, then at the doorway, then at his wrist again.

At two minutes past two the doorway let out a heavy man in a black overcoat carrying a gladstone bag in his left hand. He did not shut the door behind him. He crossed the wet sidewalk, set the bag on the running-board, and stood with his back to the sedan looking up at the third-floor windows. One window was lighted. A shadow crossed it and did not come back.

The man in the cap said: "Get in."

"In a minute."

"Get in, Pauly."

The heavy man picked up the bag and got in. The sedan went north to Lake Street and turned east under the elevated tracks.

Delaney came up from Van Buren at half-past two with his hat pulled down and his hands in his pockets. A patrolman stood in the lighted doorway chewing a toothpick.

Delaney said: "Somebody phoned me. Rosen's furs."

"Third floor. They cut the wire in the hall." The patrolman moved his shoulder out of the way. "Watchman's up there with a split head."

Delaney went up. The corridor smelled of wet plaster and cold cigars. In room 314 a grey-haired man sat on a packing case holding a folded towel against his temple, and a young man in shirtsleeves held a flashlight over an open safe. Forty-one pelts hung on a rail along the north wall. Nine hooks were empty.

"How many were there?" Delaney asked.

"Two." The watchman took the towel down and looked at it. "One of them called the other one by name."

"What name?"

"Pauly."

Delaney lit a cigarette and put the burnt match in his coat pocket. He walked to the rail, lifted the end of a silver fox skin, let it fall, and turned around.

"Say it again," he said.
