In my second autumn I built a wall of loose stones along the north edge of my field, and I have done nothing better with these hands. The stones lay where the ice had left them, some of them no bigger than a loaf and some of them such that two men were needed to stir them, and I hauled them on a sled over frozen ground, for a sled asks nothing of a horse that a man is not able to give it. Two hundred and forty feet of wall were laid in eleven days. It cost me my dinner and the loan of a crowbar, which I returned; but my neighbor priced the same length at nine dollars, and thought me poor because I would not pay him.

There is no mortar in such a wall. Every stone is held down by the weight of the stone above it, and if you set one wrong the frost will tell you of it in April, being a stricter inspector than any that I could hire. Cato bids the master sell his old oxen and his worn cart, and I have read the page in the field with numb fingers, and wondered that he nowhere thinks to say what a man should keep.

A wall keeps nothing in or out. The fox goes through it, the woodchuck lives beneath it, the bluebird comes to sit on the cap-stone and sing, and the blackberry rises in the chinks and is fed by the shade of it. Pasture and rock were here before, and pasture and rock they are still. Such a fence is only a line drawn to show where one man's opinion of the land leaves off and his neighbor's begins.

Yet I would rather build than buy. In building I came to know the bedding and the grain of every stone on my own ground; in buying I should have come to know the price of labor, or of an hour, and no more. You who pay nine dollars for your wall, what have you when the frost is done with it? The stones were here before the deed was written, and as soon as the paper is rags they will lie in some other order.
