I got out at Dinant at twenty past four, and I had the whole thing clear in my head before the train had left the platform.

It came to this. The consignment notes I had copied in the office at Antwerp gave four wagons of pit-props going up the Meuse to a sawmill at Givet, and going up every Thursday since the middle of February. Pit-props do not travel from a country with no mines to a country with plenty of them. I had said as much to Rennie, and Rennie had laughed at me and told me to mind the ledgers. So here I was with a day's leave and no business whatever on the Belgian side of the frontier.

Givet is about eighteen miles from Dinant by the river road, and the road keeps the water the whole way. That was no good to me. A man walking a towpath in a town suit is remembered by every lock-keeper he passes. But there is a track along the top of the ridge on the east bank, through the beech woods above Anseremme and Hastière, and it comes down to the road again a mile short of the frontier post. Call it twenty-two miles and five hours, which put me at the customs house about half-past nine, with the light gone.

I bought bread and a sausage and a bottle of beer at a shop by the bridge, and started up through the town.

The climb out of the valley took me forty minutes and left me wet through, and I sat on a stone at the top and ate half the bread. Below me the Meuse went round its bend in a slow grey loop, with a string of barges on it, and beyond the barges the roofs of the town, and the citadel on its rock above them. It was a fine view and I did not enjoy it. I had left my bag at the station, which meant that I had to come back for it or lose it, and losing it meant losing the copies.

I worked out that I could not have it both ways. Either the notes stayed in the bag and I went back, or they came with me and I walked into the customs house at Givet carrying the one thing I could not explain.

I put them in my sock and went on into the trees.
