Now it must be told of Thorgils, Bard's son, who dwelt at Sandwater under the ridge. He was a rich man and a quiet one, and had few words with his neighbours. His wife was named Steinvor; she was Ketil's daughter, and men said she was the best housewife in the dale.

There was a man named Grim the Grey. He dwelt north at Longholt, and owned the foreshore beyond the creek, and he was well to do but grasping, and he had two grown sons, Ospak and Arnor.

That autumn a whale drove ashore on the sand between the two farms, and both men laid claim to it. Thorgils sent his housecarle to Longholt to ask how the matter should be settled, and Grim answered him thus--

"Tell thy master the drift is mine, and the tide knows it if he does not."

The housecarle rode home and told the words as they were spoken. Then Steinvor said--

"That is an answer worth keeping in mind."

"So it is," said Thorgils; "but a whale is dear meat, and men have been slain for less. I will name witnesses, and have the old boundary told over by those who remember where it ran."

He did so, and the men of the dale bore witness that the sand was his down to the creek mouth. But before the flitting was done, Grim's sons came in the night with six men and cut away the greater part of the meat, and carried it off in two boats. Thorgils rode out at daybreak and saw the bones. He said nothing to his men, but turned home, and summoned Grim to the Thing for theft.

At the Thing many were on Thorgils' side, for Grim was little loved. Then Grim's kinsmen came and offered atonement, and it was taken. Grim was to pay ten hundreds in wadmal, and Ospak was made an outlaw for three winters, but Arnor went free because he had been at the sheep-fold that night. So they rode home, and Thorgils asked his wife whether she thought the award a good one.

"It is a good award," she said, "but Ospak will come back in three winters, and thou wilt still live at Sandwater."
